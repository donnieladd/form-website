# form. — Talking Points

Written to be **said out loud** to someone who has never heard of form.

The rule for this whole document: **never say an abstract noun without immediately
saying the concrete thing.** "Operational infrastructure" means nothing to a buyer.
"A written checklist your staff opens on Sunday morning" means something.

> 🔶 = a number or claim **you need to confirm** before saying it publicly.

---

# PART 1 — Answering "so what do you do?"

## The 15-second answer

> **"You know how every business has stuff that only works because one person
> remembers how to do it? I build the systems that make it work without that person.
> Sometimes that's software, sometimes it's a written process, sometimes it's hiring
> the right person and building their onboarding."**

Why this works: it names a problem they've personally lived, before it names a service.

**Even shorter, if they're busy:**
> "I fix the operations behind a business — with software when software is the answer,
> and with process when it isn't."

## The 60-second answer

> "Most companies I walk into don't have an idea problem. They have a *'how does this
> actually get done every week'* problem.
>
> The vision is fine. The people are good. But the way the work gets done lives in
> somebody's head, or in a spreadsheet only one person understands, or in a group chat.
> So it breaks when that person is out, when volume doubles, or when somebody quits.
>
> I come in, figure out how the work actually happens — not how the org chart says it
> happens — and then I build the thing that makes it repeatable. That might be custom
> software. It might be a documented process with a name next to every step. It might
> be a hiring pipeline. Usually it's a combination.
>
> Then I either hand it to you with documentation, or I stay on and run it."

## If they say "so… you're a consultant?"

> **"No — consultants hand you a strategy and leave. I build the thing and it works
> when I'm gone. I came up running operations, so I've been the person who had to
> live with the system after the consultant left."**

## If they say "so… you're a software developer?"

> **"I build software, but that's the last step, not the first. Plenty of the time the
> honest answer is you don't need an app, you need three people to agree on who owns
> what. I'll tell you that instead of selling you a build."**

# PART 2 — "OK, but what would you actually DO for me?"

This is the section that closes. Lead with the scenario, not the service name.

### Scenario: "Everything depends on one person"

**What they say:** *"If Sarah's out, we're stuck. She's the only one who knows how to
run payroll / close the month / set up a new client."*

**What I actually build:**
- I sit with Sarah and watch her do it — the real version, not the version in the manual
- I write it down as a step-by-step procedure, with a named owner on every step
- I build the checklist your team opens and follows on the day
- Where a step is repetitive, I automate it so software does it instead of a person

**What you get in your hands:** A written procedure, a checklist, an owner map, and
usually one or two automations. Somebody new can do Sarah's job on their second week.

---

### Scenario: "We can't find/keep the right people"

**What they say:** *"We've had this role open five months."* or *"We hire people and
they're lost for three months."*

**What I actually build:**
- Run the actual search — I have a live executive search practice, 200+ placements
- Build the hiring pipeline: where candidates come from, who screens, what the
  scorecard is, how fast you move
- Build the first-90-days system so a new hire is productive instead of guessing

**What you get in your hands:** Filled roles, plus the repeatable pipeline and an
onboarding track so the next hire doesn't start from zero.

---

### Scenario: "Our numbers are a mess"

**What they say:** *"I don't actually know if we made money last month."* or
*"Only our bookkeeper understands the spreadsheet."*

**What I actually build:**
- A clean budget structure — what the categories are, who can spend, what gets approved
- A reporting format where the numbers are stated plainly instead of buried in formulas
- The trail: every dollar has a stated disposition, so when someone asks "where did
  this go," there's an answer

**What you get in your hands:** A budget system and a monthly report you can actually
read, that holds up if a board or an auditor asks questions.

---

### Scenario: "We need to train people and we keep doing it one-on-one"

**What they say:** *"Every new person, I explain the same thing for three hours."*

**What I actually build:**
- Take what's in your head and turn it into a real course: a sequence, lessons,
  and a way to check whether they actually learned it
- Built so it scales — certification tracks, onboarding curriculum, training programs

**What you get in your hands:** A curriculum someone can move through without you in
the room, and a way to verify they're ready.

# PART 4 — What working together actually looks like

Walk them through it. Removes the fear of the unknown.

**1 · A conversation (free).** I ask what's actually breaking. Not a pitch — I'm
diagnosing. Most people describe a symptom; I'm looking for the mechanism underneath.

**2 · I tell you what I think it is.** Sometimes the honest answer is "that's a
two-week fix, here's what it costs." Sometimes it's "this is bigger than you think
and here's why."

**3 · A written scope.** What gets built, what it costs, when it's done, what I need
from you. No surprises later.

**4 · I build it.** You see it early and often, not at the end. It gets tested under
real conditions with real users before I call it done.

**5 · Handoff — or I stay.** You get the system plus documentation to run it without
me. Or, if the work is continuous, I stay on retainer. That's *Continuum*.

🔶 **Typical timeline / typical starting cost** — *fill these in. A buyer will ask
"how long" and "how much" in the first five minutes, and "it depends" loses the room.
Have a real bracket ready, even a wide one.*

# PART 5 — Money

**form. does not publish rates.** Nothing on the site carries a number, and that
is deliberate — it's how firms at this level operate. Pricing is set in a written
proposal after the problem is understood.

**When they ask "what does it cost?" — and they will, early:**
> "I don't quote before I understand the problem. If I did, I'd either overcharge
> you or underestimate the work, and both of those end badly. Give me one
> conversation and you'll have a real number in writing."

That answer is not evasion — it's the same reason you don't publish. Say it with
confidence and it reads as standard practice, because it is.

**If they push a second time** (some will, to test you):
> "Fair. What I can tell you is how it's structured: monthly retainer for ongoing
> work, fixed scope for a defined build, and everything in writing before anything
> starts. The number depends on the size of the operation — tell me about yours."

**What NOT to do:** don't name a figure to fill a silence, don't give a "ballpark"
you'd have to walk back, and don't apologise for not having a price sheet. The
absence of published pricing is a signal, not a gap.

**On retainer vs. project:**
> "If you've got one clear problem — a site, a budget rebuild, a course — that's a
> project, and a project is cheaper and faster. A retainer only earns its cost when
> the work is continuous. If it isn't yet, I'll tell you."

# PART 6 — Objection handling

**"We already have an agency / a developer."**
> "Good — I'm usually not replacing them. They make the thing look right. I make sure
> the operation behind it runs. Different problem."

**"We're too small for this."**
> "Then you're exactly who this helps most — you don't have a department to absorb the
> chaos. And the fix is usually smaller than you think. Sometimes it's one process."

**"How do I know you can do this?"**
> "Ask me about a problem you're actually having right now, and I'll tell you how I'd
> approach it. That'll tell you more than a case study."

**"What if it doesn't work?"**
> "Nothing gets called done because it worked in a demo. It goes in front of real users
> under real conditions first. And you own the documentation either way — you're never
> locked to me to keep it running."

**"Can't AI just do this?"**
> "Some of it, yes — and where it can, I use it. But AI doesn't fix a process nobody
> agreed on. It just does the wrong thing faster. The judgment about *what* to automate
> is the actual work."

# PART 7 — What I don't do

Say this early and unprompted. It's the single most credible thing on the list.

> **"I don't do audit, tax, legal, or regulatory work — that needs licensure I don't
> hold. I don't run enterprise cloud infrastructure. I'm not going to tell you I have
> a hundred people, because I don't. What I do is a defined set of things I can
> actually deliver."**

The principle: **no borrowed authority.** No invented statistics, no logos not earned,
no scale not built. The record speaks at its actual size.

# PART 9 — Closers

Pick one. Don't stack them.

- **"Bring me the hard one."** The problem that's been on the list two quarters that
  nobody has a clean answer for. That's the conversation worth having.
- **"Start with one thing."** One process, one budget, one role, one course. Tell me
  which and I'll scope it honestly.
- **"Vision needs structure."** Everybody has the vision. I build the structure.
